Severe class imbalance in threshold-mediated deployments creates an evaluative blind spot: models with acceptable conventional performance can still differ sharply in minority accessibility as policy thresholds change. We study this phenomenon using reachability trajectories, a minority-conditioned threshold-evolution lens that tracks operational accessibility beyond single-threshold recall. Across family-level comparisons and fixed-architecture neural perturbations, current evidence suggests AUROC, average precision, and calibration summaries are partially non-equivalent to threshold-mediated accessibility behavior in this setting. We further observe that reliability improvements can coincide with lower operational smoothness and higher jump intensity, indicating that reliability and accessibility act as distinct operational axes under severe imbalance. These findings suggest that threshold-policy controllability requires trajectory-aware evaluation alongside ranking and calibration metrics. We present this as an empirical and conceptual framing contribution in a constrained severe-imbalance protocol, not a new learner, not a complete morphology theory, and not a universal taxonomy claim.
